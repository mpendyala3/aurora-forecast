          08Learn

          What the numbers mean

          Four inputs decide every aurora night. Here is how to read them.

          Kp index

          The planet-wide “storm strength” meter, from 0 (quiet) to 9 (extreme).

          Kp is a 3-hour index of how disturbed Earth’s magnetic field is, measured by a network of magnetometers on a quasi-logarithmic 0–9 scale. Kp 5 and above are geomagnetic storms, labelled G1 to G5 by NOAA.

          The higher the Kp, the further the auroral oval expands toward the equator. As a rule of thumb the aurora can be seen on the horizon down to about 66° geomagnetic latitude at Kp 0, 60° at Kp 3, 56° at Kp 5 and 52° at Kp 7.

          Aurora Hunt uses the observed Kp for right now, NOAA’s 3-day Kp forecast for the hourly chart, and the 27-day outlook for the calendar.

          Bz

          The direction of the solar wind’s magnetic field. Negative is good.

          Bz is the north–south component of the interplanetary magnetic field. When it points south (negative) it reconnects with Earth’s field and lets solar wind energy pour into the magnetosphere.

          A steady Bz of −5 nT or lower for an hour, with solar wind faster than about 500 km/s, is a classic recipe for a bright display. Short negative spikes matter less than sustained southward periods.

          Bz is measured about 1.5 million km upstream of Earth, so it is the best 15–60 minute early warning there is.

          Cloud cover

          The curtain in front of the show. Clear sky beats a strong storm.

          The aurora happens 100–300 km up — far above every cloud. Low and mid-level cloud blocks it completely; thin high cloud only softens it.

          Aurora Hunt reads total cloud cover from Open-Meteo for each hour of the night at your exact coordinates, so a two-hour clear gap shows up as a spike in the hourly chart.

          If your spot is clouded over, the “where to be tonight” list compares live cloud at the classic aurora towns.

          Darkness

          Aurora needs a properly dark sky — and that depends on season and latitude.

          The forecast calculates the Sun’s real elevation for your location. Aurora starts to show once the Sun is about 6° below the horizon and is at its best below 12° (nautical darkness).

          In midsummer the high Arctic never gets dark, so the chance stays at zero no matter how strong the storm. In midwinter the polar night can give 20 hours of viewing.

          Moonlight lowers contrast for faint aurora, so the calendar gently favours nights near the new moon.

          09FAQ

          Northern lights, answered

          Will I see the northern lights tonight?

          You need three things at once: enough geomagnetic activity for your latitude, a dark sky, and clear weather. Aurora Hunt scores all three hour by hour for your location using live NOAA and Open-Meteo data, and shows the best viewing window for tonight. Check tonight’s forecast.

          What Kp index do I need to see the aurora?

          It depends on your geomagnetic latitude. Kp 0–2 is enough under the auroral oval (Tromsø, Abisko, Iceland, Fairbanks, Yellowknife). Kp 3–4 reaches central Scandinavia, Scotland’s far north and southern Canada. Kp 5 (a G1 storm) reaches Stockholm, Oslo, Edinburgh and the northern United States. Kp 7 and above can bring aurora to northern England, Germany, Poland and the central United States.

          What is the best time of night to see the northern lights?

          Usually between about 22:00 and 02:00 local time, around magnetic midnight, when your location rotates under the most active part of the auroral oval. Aurora can appear at any time when the sky is dark, so check the hourly forecast.

          What does Bz mean in an aurora forecast?

          Bz is the north–south direction of the magnetic field carried by the solar wind. When Bz is negative (southward) it connects with Earth’s magnetic field and lets energy in, which powers the aurora. A sustained Bz of −5 nT or lower with fast solar wind is a strong sign.

          What are the best months to see the aurora?

          In the Arctic the season runs from late August to early April, when nights are dark. Geomagnetic activity is statistically highest around the equinoxes in March and September–October. Above the Arctic Circle the midnight sun hides the aurora from roughly May to July.

          Does a full moon ruin the northern lights?

          No. A bright moon washes out faint aurora and reduces contrast, but moderate and strong displays are easily visible. Cloud cover and city lights matter far more than the moon.

          How accurate are aurora forecasts?

          Short-range forecasts are the most reliable: satellites about 1.5 million km upstream of Earth measure the solar wind 15–60 minutes before it arrives. NOAA’s 3-day Kp forecast is a useful guide, and the 27-day outlook is based on the Sun’s rotation and should be treated as a tendency, not a promise.

          Where does Aurora Hunt get its data, and is it free?

          Aurora Hunt is free and needs no account. Space weather comes from the NOAA Space Weather Prediction Center (Kp index, Kp forecast, solar wind, Bz and the OVATION aurora model). Cloud cover and location search come from Open-Meteo. Everything is calculated in your browser.

          10Method

          How the forecast is made

          1. ReachYour geomagnetic latitude is compared with how far the auroral oval extends at the current and forecast Kp.
          2. EnergyLive solar wind speed and Bz nudge the score up when the Sun is actually delivering.
          3. DarknessThe Sun’s true elevation at your coordinates, minute by minute — polar night and midnight sun included.
          4. SkyHourly cloud cover for your exact location. A cloudy sky scales the chance toward zero.
          5. HonestyIf a feed is down the page says so and falls back to a quiet-sky estimate instead of inventing numbers.
